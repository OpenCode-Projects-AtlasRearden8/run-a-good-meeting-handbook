Read This First

Welcome. This handbook exists for one reason: meetings that should take 30 minutes keep taking 90, and everybody shrugs and accepts it. You don't have to.

This is a plain-English manual for running small-business and club meetings — the kind with five to fifteen people in a room (or on a video call, or around a kitchen table). It covers four things:

  1. Agendas that fit on a card. A short, honest plan for what you'll actually talk about.
  2. Minutes people actually read. Notes that capture decisions, not noise.
  3. Keeping the meeting on track. Kind ways to handle the two people who could talk all year, and the three who never say a word.
  4. A gentle AI assist. How to use an AI assistant for clean-up work — recaps, action lists — without letting it make your decisions for you.

How to use this handbook

You can read it cover to cover (it takes about 20 minutes), or just grab the templates in docs/templates/ and use them tonight. Nothing here needs special software, a paid subscription, or a permission slip from headquarters.

  • Going to a meeting tomorrow? Start with the agenda guide.
  • Dreading writing up notes? Start with the minutes guide.
  • Trying to survive the person who monologues? Start with the facilitation guide.
  • Curious about using AI for recaps? Start with the AI assist guide.

What this is not

  • Not a parliamentary-rulebook. If your organization requires Robert's Rules, keep using it; this handbook layers on top with friendlier habits.
  • Not a note-taking app. This is a manual, not software. It pairs well with any tool you already use, including the separate meeting-notes-summarizer project if you want automated summaries.
  • Not legal, financial, or medical advice. It's meeting craft, explained like a friend would.

The one rule

Almost every meeting problem traces back to one thing: no one said out loud what the meeting was for. So before anything else, every meeting gets a stated purpose ("decide X", "update on Y", "brainstorm Z") and a named person who wraps it up. Everything else in this book is detail around that habit.

That's it. Take what's useful, leave what isn't, and copy freely — everything here is MIT licensed.

Agendas That Fit on a Card

An agenda is not a document. It's a promise of what will happen, small enough that everyone actually looks at it. If it feels like homework, it's too long.

The card test

Imagine your agenda pencilled onto a 4×6 index card. If it doesn't fit (or you "need smaller handwriting"), it's a wish list, not an agenda. Fix it with the three-part structure below — everything ends up on one card.

The structure: three sections only

Every good card has three sections, in this order:

  1. Housekeeping (2 minutes) — roll call, last meeting's decisions recap, one-line treasurer/board updates.
  2. The main thing (most of the time) — the one discussion the meeting exists for: a decision to make, a problem to solve, or a plan to build.
  3. Wrap-up (5 minutes) — read the decisions aloud, confirm who does what by when, pick the next meeting date.

If you find yourself with four main things, that's two meetings. Run the smaller ones by email or quick messages, and protect the time for the one thing that really needs everyone together.

The five fields

Next to each item, note three things. This is what turns an agenda from a topic list into a working tool:

  • Who leads it — one name per item.
  • What "done" looks like — usually "we decide" or "we agree to X."
  • A time limit — even a rough one. "Fundraiser — Priya — decide budget — 15 min."

A template with these fields is in templates/agenda-card-template.md.

Circulating and starting on time

  • Send the card 24 hours ahead wherever your group already communicates (email list, group chat, pinned message). Don't chase a new channel.
  • At the start, read the purpose out loud in one sentence: "Tonight we pick the spring fundraiser and confirm the budget." This 10-second habit keeps drift in check better than any technique in the facilitation chapter.
  • Start on time. People learn within two meetings. If latecomers missed the decision, that's recorded in the minutes — you don't rewind the whole meeting.

The parking lot

Keep a running "parking lot" list for interesting topics that derail the main thing ("that reminds me of the storage room problem..."). Write them down, say "parking that, let's finish the decision", and add it to a future card. Nothing is lost — it's just rescheduled. This single habit fixes most "we ran out of time" meetings.

Common failure modes

  • The 40-item agenda. Ten items will be touched meaninglessly for two hours. Cut to three and actually finish them.
  • "Old business." Most old business is unrecorded decisions resurfacing. The minutes fix this (next chapter).
  • Discussion before the item is framed. Ask the leader to state the question first: "We need to pick between location A and B by tonight." Then talk.

You now have what you need to run a real meeting. The next skill is writing the notes people will actually read — that's coming up.

Minutes People Actually Read

Most minutes are unreadable because they try to be a transcript. Minutes are not a recording — they're a receipt. What was decided, who's doing what, by when. Read receipts, not diaries.

The secret: decide what you're keeping

Minutes serve exactly four questions. If a line of writing doesn't answer one of them, it doesn't belong in the minutes:

  1. What did we decide? Verbatim enough that a member who missed the meeting knows what changed.
  2. What happens next? Action items with a name and a date ("Dana books the hall by Friday").
  3. What did we table? The parking lot, so nothing dies by forgetting.
  4. Who was there? Attendance, so future-you knows who was in the room when the vote happened.

Everything else — the spirited three-minute debate, the side jokes, the "so anyway, as I was saying" — is not minutes. It's memory. Let it stay in the room.

During the meeting: capture, don't compose

  • Take bullet points, not paragraphs. Minutes get composed after, in five quiet minutes.
  • Each time a decision happens, physically say or type a marker: "Decision: we'll spend up to $200 on venue." This becomes the minutes and also becomes the read-back at wrap-up (see facilitation guide).
  • Actions bubble up in real time: when someone says "I'll email her", flag it the moment it's said — don't trust yourself to catch it later.
  • You don't need to identify every speaker. "The group discussed venues... motion to cap spend passed" reads fine. Only names matter for motions, votes, and action items.

After the meeting: the five-minute rule

Within a day (ideally within the hour), spend five minutes turning bullets into the template: date, attendees, decisions, actions, tabled items, next meeting. Then send it once, to the same channel as the agenda, and — the crucial move — start the next meeting's card from it. "Old business" items come from the previous minutes' action list. That's the loop that makes minutes earn their keep.

The template's in templates/minutes-template.md, including a filled-in sample (templates/minutes-sample.md).

Formatting rules that make minutes readable

  • Bold the decisions. The eye should be able to scan decisions in ten seconds.
  • One line per action, with the name first: "Dana — book hall — by Fri."
  • No walls of text. If a paragraph is more than three sentences, ask whether it's answering one of the four questions.
  • Subject line matters: "Minutes — Purple Lens Club — Nov 4 (venue decided: $200 cap)" tells members whether they need to open it. Vague subject = unread.

Sensitive stuff

Discipline, personnel, money disputes, anything mentioning a specific person's private life: keep it to a factual minimum ("Executive session held; no action taken") — not a characterization. This handbook is not legal advice, but the plain rule is: minutes are records people may read years later, including outsiders. Write like a careful, kind, neutral observer.

Who takes minutes

Rotate. The same person doing it forever is how minutes become one person's private chore (and eventual resentment). Put "minutes: Dana" at the top of the agenda card so it's assigned before the meeting starts, not negotiated at the end.

Keeping the Meeting On Track (Kindly)

Facilitation sounds like it needs a certificate. It doesn't. It's mostly paying attention to the room and asking small, harmless questions. This chapter covers the patterns that actually come up in small-business and club meetings, and gentle lines you can borrow word-for-word.

Your job if you're the facilitator

You're not the boss of the conversation. You're its practical host: you keep the agreed scope visible and make sure the quieter half of the room gets air time. You don't need to be the most senior person, or the loudest, or even an officer — you just need to be willing to say "let's park that and come back to the decision."

The seven situations (and lines that work)

1. The person who takes every thread.

They're usually not doing it to dominate — they're processing out loud. Cut in at a breath with appreciation plus redirect:

"That's a great thread — let's put it in the parking lot so we can finish the venue decision first."

If they came to discuss their topic, and it's not on the card, that's not a failure of the meeting. Offer them an agenda item at the next one.

2. The point that won't land.

Someone has made their case well the third time. Say it plainly but warmly:

"I think we've heard you on this — is anyone going to change their vote?" If not: "Sounds like we're decided, moving on."

3. The person who never speaks.

Don't put them on the spot with "What do you think, Sam?" in front of everyone — for many people that's panic, not participation. Instead:

  • Go around the room ("Let's hear one idea from everyone, even 'I have none'") — structured turn-taking gives quiet folks an easy slot.
  • Ask narrow, factual questions first: "Sam, does the $200 figure cover the deposit?" Concrete beats abstract.
  • If they message you a good idea after the meeting, read it into the next meeting with credit. It teaches the group that quiet contributions count.

4. The topic that opens like a jack-in-the-box.

You're ten minutes deep before someone says: "Wait, are we allowed to spend money?" Stop and reframe before going further: "Good catch — do we decide tonight, or does this go to the board item?" Decide the frame, then resume.

5. Silence after "any questions?"

Silence is not agreement; it's usually digestion. Give a beat, then convert: "OK — what's blocking anyone from saying yes?" Framing as "what's blocking" is easier to answer than "any questions?"

6. The vote that nobody is sure about.

Repeat the exact proposal before voting — "Just to be clear: we cap venue spend at $200, Dana will confirm two options by Friday, we finalize next meeting. Show of hands?" Then state the result out loud: "That's 7–2, motion passes." The minutes are writing themselves while you do this.

7. The meeting that's run out of time.

End early rather than sloppy. Read back decisions and actions, name what's moving to the parking lot, set the next date. Leaving with 10 minutes left and a clean wrap is a successful meeting, not a failure of nerve.

If you're not facilitating, you can still do this

Any member can say "can we park that until after the decision?" — it works better than you'd think when it's a regular member, not the chair. Group health isn't the chair's sole responsibility; it's everyone's a little.

A note on tone

Gentle redirection works because it signals: your point is heard, and we're going to finish what we promised. No sarcasm, no raised voice, no public embarrassment of the rambler. The ideal outcome is that the talkative person keeps contributing — just in the right slots — and the quiet folks keep coming back. If someone leaves the meeting feeling ambushed, you did it wrong even if the minutes are immaculate.

A Gentle AI Assist for Recaps

This handbook is deliberately tool-agnostic. Whatever AI assistant you use, the pattern below stays the same: AI does the tidy-up labor, humans keep the judgment. Used this way, AI is a helpful typist — not the president of your club.

The rule of thumbs

  1. Humans decide; AI tidies. Never let an AI output become a decision on its own — you review every line before anyone acts on it.
  2. Privacy first. Strip names (except action-item owners the group has already published), financial figures you're not comfortable sharing, and anything sensitive before you paste text into any tool. If your group discusses members' private matters, don't feed those parts in at all. If your tool records audio, only record with everyone's clear okay and check your group's bylaws.
  3. Verify easy things yourself. Dates, names, dollar amounts: 10 seconds of checking beats an error living in your minutes for a year.

Use 1: Bullets into a readable recap

You took rough bullet notes during the meeting (see the minutes chapter). Paste them into your AI assistant with a prompt like this — it's the one used for this handbook's own sample:

Here are rough meeting notes from our club meeting. Turn them into minutes
using this structure only — do not invent anything not in the notes, and if
something is unclear, write [CHECK: your best guess]. Keep it under one page. Structure:
- Date & attendees
- Decisions (bold)
- Action items (name — task — due date)
- Tabled / parking lot
- Next meeting

Notes: [paste notes]

The phrases doing the real work are "do not invent anything" and "write [CHECK]" — they turn a confident guesser into an honest assistant that flags holes instead of papering over them. Fill the [CHECK] items yourself from memory.

Use 2: Meeting notes into a friendly announcement

Minutes are for the record; announcements get read. Ask:

Write a 5-sentence email to club members based on these minutes: warm in
tone, front-loads the one big decision, ends with who needs to do what by
when. Keep our informal voice, no corporate phrases.

Is that deceptive? No more than using a nicer font. The content is your minutes; the assistant is doing layout. You still proofread and sign it yourself.

Use 3: The decision history

Paste the last three meetings' minutes and ask:

List every open action item across these minutes, grouped by owner,
with the latest due date mentioned for each. Flag any that appear
twice with different dates.

That last flag ("appears twice with different dates") catches the classic club bug: the same task carried forward with quietly drifting deadlines. AI is genuinely good at this boring cross-referencing — it's the best non-creative use on this list.

Use 4: Agenda help

At the start of the cycle: "Here are last month's minutes. Draft next meeting's agenda card: housekeeping, one main item (the open action items suggest the main thing is X), and questions to ask to close each open action." Treat the result as a first draft — your agenda, your judgment on what matters.

What not to hand to AI

  • Raw recordings of sensitive discussions. (Personnel, discipline, personal finances of members.)
  • Voting records you intend to keep private — some orgs publish them, some don't; know which you are before pasting.
  • Anything where being wrong matters a lot. Financial statements, legal notices, bylaws amendments: draft by hand, use AI only to check tone or clarity.

The honest version

AI makes recaps 80% faster because 80% of recap work is mechanical reorganization. The remaining 20% — judgment about what matters, what's fair, what's kind — is yours, and that's the good news: it stays that way.

Agenda Card Template

Copy this into whatever your group uses to communicate. If it can't fit on an index card, cut items — not font size.

MEETING CARD — [GROUP NAME]
[Date] · [Start time] · [Location / video link]
Purpose in one sentence: [Tonight/Today we ____ ]
Facilitator: [name]   Minutes: [name]   Timekeeper: [name or "chair"]

HOUSEKEEPING (5 min)
- [ ] Attendance & welcome new folks
- [ ] Recap of last meeting's decisions (minutes: [link or read-aloud])
- [ ] Quick reports (treasurer, officers) — 1 min each, updates only

THE MAIN THING ([__] min)
[ ] [Item 1 title] — [who leads] — [decide / discuss / plan] — [time]
    Question to answer: [Exactly what are we deciding or solving?]

WRAP-UP (5 min)
- [ ] Read back: decisions + who does what by when
- [ ] Parking lot review (add new items here, don't debate them)
- [ ] Next meeting date & who's on the card

PARKING LOT (from last time)
- [item] — [owner or "someday"]

BEFORE YOU SEND: Does the "main thing" have a deadline tonight?
Anything not needed by everyone? → handle by email/chat instead.

Minutes Template

Fill this in within a day of the meeting. If a section is empty, delete it — blank scaffolding makes minutes look unfinished.

MINUTES — [GROUP NAME]
Date: [date]        Location/link: [where]
Facilitator: [name]  Minutes taken by: [name]

ATTENDEES
[present] · Absent: [names, or "—"]

DECISIONS MADE
- **[Decision 1 — stated so a member who was absent understands what changed]**
  (approved [X–Y] / by consensus / carried over from [date])
- **[Decision 2]**

ACTION ITEMS
- [Name] — [task] — due [date]
- [Name] — [task] — due [date]

TABLED / PARKING LOT
- [item] — [owner or "someday"]

NOTABLE DISCUSSION (2–4 bullets max, only if it aids future decisions)
- [the one fact or constraint people will need to remember]

NEXT MEETING
[date, time, place, who takes minutes]

(Financial note if applicable: balance as of [date]: [amount]; changes: [what and why].)

Rules baked into this template

  • Decisions are bold. Scannable in ten seconds.
  • Actions have owner + date or they're not actions — they're wishes.
  • Discussion is optional. Keep only what a future decision will need.
  • The subject line when you send it: Minutes — [group] — [date] — [one-line headline].

A filled-in example is in minutes-sample.md.
